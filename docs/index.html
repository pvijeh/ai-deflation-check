Is AI "obviously deflationary"?

A check against public data: what people actually pay, what software sellers actually receive, how often the biggest services break, and how much the buildout itself costs. No CPI anywhere. Cut line is November 2022, ChatGPT's release. Everything here is regenerated by the repo; the write-up with sources is FINDINGS.md.

Executive summary

What can and cannot be shown.

  • Nothing here proves AI caused anything. The "after" period (Nov 2022 on) also contains the fastest rate rises in 40 years, the end of the pandemic price spike, tariffs, and the 2023 tech layoffs. With three years of data and one cut date, those cannot be separated from AI. Every chart is timing evidence, not causal evidence.
  • What can be tested is the claim's word "obviously": if the effect were large and already here, it should be visible somewhere in what buyers pay. It is not.

The case, on the evidence so far: not deflationary for buyers.

  • Every price buyers pay that we can track without CPI is still rising after 2022: rents, home prices, streaming, electricity, SaaS list prices. The series that fell (world food, energy) fell for pre-AI reasons.
  • Software sellers raised prices and widened margins. Incumbents sell AI as a paid add-on; the collapse in LLM token cost has stayed with vendors and chipmakers.
  • The buildout added roughly 0.7–1.3% of GDP of new spending in 2025. Offsetting that would need a ~3% drop in all US goods prices. Nothing like it is visible.
  • The one place the hypothesis gets support is the difference tests: AI-exposed industries' output per hour and relative prices moved the predicted way after 2022. That is also exactly what layoffs would produce, and it has not reached buyers. If AI turns deflationary, this is where it will show first.

Why each metric is here.

  • Non-CPI prices (Zillow, Case-Shiller, FAO, World Bank, EIA, streaming list prices): the claim is about prices, and the brief was to avoid the CPI. These are transaction- or list-price based and mostly private.
  • Software producer prices (BLS PPI): the only long series of what software sellers receive, back to 1997. Government-reported, so shown as a comparison line, not primary evidence.
  • SEC 10-K panel (32 software companies): tests the counter-story that AI competition raises vendor costs. Audited numbers; margins and cost ratios answer it directly.
  • SaaS and LLM list prices: whether software buyers pay less, and whether the one input that did deflate (tokens) passed through.
  • Status-page incidents: a quality proxy. If AI improved software, the biggest services should break less. Disclosure counts, not uptime, and read with that caveat.
  • Hyperscaler capex and NVIDIA revenue: the other side of the ledger. "Deflationary" has to be net of the spending the buildout itself adds; this sizes it and puts it against past buildouts.
  • Exposed vs unexposed productivity and prices, US vs peers: differences within one economy or across similar ones cancel the shared macro noise. The closest this data can get to isolating an AI effect.

Every number is regenerated from public sources by the scripts in github.com/pvijeh/ai-deflation-check; raw downloads in data/raw, cleaned series in data/processed, sources and method in FINDINGS.md.

1. Prices people pay rebased Dec 2019 = 100, no CPI

If AI were pushing prices down, some of these should bend after the dashed line. Housing, rent, electricity and streaming keep rising. World food and energy commodities fell, from a 2022 spike that predates any AI footprint. Software producer prices, flat-to-down for 25 years, turned up.

Sources: Zillow ZHVI/ZORI; S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller; FAO Food Price Index; World Bank Commodity Price Data; EIA Electric Power Monthly table 5.3; BLS Producer Price Index (what sellers receive; a government-reported index, shown as a comparison line rather than as primary evidence) via FRED; streaming list prices hand-compiled from vendor announcements (equal-weight Netflix Standard, Spotify Premium, Hulu with ads, YouTube Premium, Amazon Prime/12).

2. Software vendors 32 US-listed companies, SEC 10-K, FY2015–2025

The counter-story was that AI raises vendor costs through competition. The filings show the opposite: gross margins up, R&D and sales & marketing down as a share of revenue, operating margins from break-even to double digits. Sellers raised prices and cut cost ratios. Meanwhile the one input that did deflate, LLM tokens, fell ~90%.

Median company shown; revenue-weighted aggregates (ex Microsoft/Oracle) in the repo tables. Oracle excluded from gross margin (no total cost-of-revenue tag). LLM prices: 75/25 input/output blend per 1M tokens, log scale, from vendor pricing pages.

3. Reliability status-page incidents, excluding maintenance

Quality proxy. Cloudflare and GitHub, the two providers with an unchanged public feed for the whole decade, roughly doubled their reported incidents after 2022; GitHub's major-severity count went from 1 to 36 a year. Datadog improved. Nothing shows reliability getting better.

Counts are what providers chose to post, not measured downtime. AWS is reconstructed from Internet Archive copies of the old status dashboard and ends March 2023. Google Cloud's feed changed shape in 2021 and shrank in 2025, so it is shown for 2021–2024 only in the comparison.

4. The other side of the ledger capex from 10-K cash-flow statements

The buildout is itself new spending. Five companies' capex above their 2022 level was $224bn in FY2025: 0.7% of GDP, or 3.4% of everything Americans spend on goods. For the net effect to be deflationary, prices elsewhere have to fall by at least that much. They have not.

Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle: PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment (Amazon: ProductiveAssets) from SEC XBRL, fiscal years. Denominators: BEA nominal PCE goods, PCE, GDP via FRED, annual averages. 0.7% is a lower bound with a specific definition; a range of 0.7–1.3% is argued in the write-up. Historical comparison: railroads ~2.4% of GDP/yr (1865–90), electrification 1.1%, highways 1.6%, telecom 0.8–1.0% (NY Fed, Apr 2026).

5. Is the effect visible where it should be? differences, so the macro noise cancels

Aggregate productivity is too noisy to show a 0.7%-of-GDP buildout. But if AI works, the industries that use it most should pull away from the ones that barely can, inside the same economy. They do, after 2022, and their relative prices soften. Nothing here separates that from the 2023 layoffs, and none of it has reached the prices buyers pay above.

Government-produced series (BLS detailed-industry labor productivity and PPI; OECD GDP per hour), used here for output per hour and cross-country comparison, not as the primary evidence on prices. Groups are medians of industry indexes rebased to 2019, balanced to the series that run to the latest year: exposed productivity is five industries (software publishers, banking, engineering, publishing, travel agencies; the CPA, tax, architecture, advertising and staffing series stop at 2023). Travel agencies' +62% is a COVID rebound.

Where this leaves the claim

  • No price consumers or business buyers pay, tracked here without CPI, is falling after 2022. Commodities that fell did so for pre-AI reasons.
  • Software sellers' prices rose after 25 years of drift down; incumbent SaaS list prices rose, with AI sold as a paid add-on.
  • Vendor margins widened. "Competition raising costs" is not what incumbents' filings show; pricing power is.
  • Reliability, where measurable, got worse or stayed flat.
  • AI-exposed industries' output per hour did pull away from unexposed ones after 2022, and their relative producer prices drifted down about 1pp/yr. That is the pattern the hypothesis predicts, but it is also the pattern the 2023 layoffs predict, and it has not shown up as price cuts.
  • The buildout added roughly 0.7–1.3% of GDP in new spending in 2025. The only strongly deflating price is the LLM token, an input; so far that has gone to vendor margin and hardware, not to buyers.

What would change this: panel gross margins turning down as inference costs bite; software PPI rolling over; incident rates at Cloudflare/GitHub falling back; or output prices in AI-exposed industries decelerating relative to unexposed ones. Three years of "after" data is not much. The scripts re-run.
