# Is AI "obviously deflationary"? What the public data says so far

Cut date used throughout: November 2022 (ChatGPT release). "Before" is 2019–2022, "after" is 2023 to the latest data. Every number below is regenerated by `make all`; the tables it prints are in `data/processed/summary_*.csv`.

## Executive summary

What can and cannot be shown:

- Nothing here proves AI caused anything. The "after" period also contains the fastest rate rises in 40 years, the unwinding of the pandemic price spike, tariffs, and the 2023 tech layoffs. With three years of data and one cut date, those cannot be separated from AI. Every chart is timing evidence, not causal evidence.
- What can be tested is the word "obviously": if the effect were large and already here, it should be visible somewhere in what buyers pay. It is not.

The case, on the evidence so far: not deflationary for buyers.

- Every price buyers pay that we can track without CPI is still rising after 2022: rents, home prices, streaming, electricity, SaaS list prices. The series that fell (world food, energy) fell for pre-AI reasons.
- Software sellers raised prices and widened margins. Incumbents sell AI as a paid add-on; the collapse in LLM token cost has stayed with vendors and chipmakers.
- The buildout added roughly 0.7–1.3% of GDP of new spending in 2025. Offsetting that would need a ~3% drop in all US goods prices. Nothing like it is visible.
- The one place the hypothesis gets support is the difference tests (section 5): AI-exposed industries' output per hour and relative prices moved the predicted way after 2022. That is also exactly what layoffs would produce, and it has not reached buyers. If AI turns deflationary, this is where it will show first.

Why each metric is here:

- Non-CPI prices (Zillow, Case-Shiller, FAO, World Bank, EIA, streaming list prices): the claim is about prices, and the brief was to avoid the CPI. Transaction- or list-price based, mostly private.
- Software producer prices (BLS PPI): the only long series of what software sellers receive, back to 1997. Government-reported, so a comparison line, not primary evidence.
- SEC 10-K panel, 32 software companies: tests the counter-story that AI competition raises vendor costs. Audited numbers; margins and cost ratios answer it directly.
- SaaS and LLM list prices: whether software buyers pay less, and whether the one input that did deflate (tokens) passed through.
- Status-page incidents: a quality proxy. If AI improved software, the biggest services should break less. Disclosure counts, not uptime.
- Hyperscaler capex and NVIDIA revenue: the other side of the ledger. "Deflationary" has to be net of the spending the buildout adds; this sizes it against past buildouts.
- Exposed vs unexposed productivity and prices, US vs peers: differences within one economy or across similar ones cancel the shared macro noise. The closest this data gets to isolating an AI effect.

Data and code: [github.com/pvijeh/ai-deflation-check](https://github.com/pvijeh/ai-deflation-check) — raw downloads in [`data/raw`](https://github.com/pvijeh/ai-deflation-check/tree/main/data/raw), cleaned series in [`data/processed`](https://github.com/pvijeh/ai-deflation-check/tree/main/data/processed), site at [pvijeh.github.io/ai-deflation-check](https://pvijeh.github.io/ai-deflation-check/).

## The claim and how we tried to break it

If AI were obviously deflationary, at least one of these should show up in public data by now:

1. Prices of things people buy should be growing more slowly or falling — measured without the CPI.
2. Software vendors should be delivering more for less: prices received by software sellers flat or down, and cost-to-serve (gross margin) improving.
3. Software quality, proxied by the reliability of the biggest infrastructure providers, should be improving, or at least not getting worse.
4. Since the buildout is itself a large addition to spending, the price declines it causes elsewhere have to be at least that large for the net effect to be deflationary. We compute how large that is.

The counter-story to test: AI mostly raises competition and cost inside the software industry (more R&D, more marketing, more compute) with little or questionable quality gain, and prices to buyers go up, not down.

What this does and does not say:

- Home prices and rents are still rising after 2022, just slower than in the 2020–22 spike. Nothing here is falling.
- World food and energy commodity prices did fall from their 2022 peaks. That is the reversal of a war-and-pandemic spike, and it started before generative AI had any commercial footprint; it is not evidence for AI.
- Metals, and especially precious metals, are up sharply. Data-centre buildout is a plausible contributor to copper and industrial metal demand, but this data cannot attribute it.
- US electricity prices accelerated after 2020 and kept rising through 2025. Residential went from 13.2¢/kWh (2020) to 17.3¢ (2025). This is the one consumer price where the AI buildout is a commonly claimed driver; the data is consistent with that but does not prove it.
- Streaming list prices rose ~4%/yr both before and after; no change in trend. Every service in the basket raised its price at least once after Nov 2022.
- The producer price index for software publishers drifted *down* 14% over 25 years (Dec 1997 = 100, Nov 2022 = 86) and then rose 14–17% in the three years after ChatGPT (peak 101 in late 2025, 98 in Aug 2026). Software was the deflationary sector for a quarter-century; after 2022 it stopped being one. It is a government-reported index (BLS), so it is kept here as a comparison line; the private evidence pointing the same way is the SaaS list-price table and the SEC margin panel in section 2.

## 2. Software vendor economics (chart: `charts/3_vendor_costs.png`)

Panel: 32 US-listed software companies with complete FY2019–2025 10-K data (list in `scripts/fetch_sec.py`). Values from SEC XBRL company facts.

Median company, percent of revenue:

| FY | gross margin | R&D | sales & marketing | operating margin | stock comp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 72.4 | 21.1 | 42.9 | -3.4 | 11.2 |
| 2022 | 74.9 | 24.0 | 35.5 | -0.1 | 13.5 |
| 2023 | 75.6 | 23.0 | 34.2 | 5.5 | 16.3 |
| 2025 | 77.0 | 21.9 | 31.6 | 14.5 | 15.0 |

- Gross margins did not compress. Median rose ~2 points since 2022. If AI inference costs were eating into cost-to-serve, it does not show up at the panel level yet.
- R&D as a share of revenue peaked in FY2022 and has come down. Sales & marketing share fell steadily. Operating margins jumped from roughly break-even to double digits.
- Reading: the 2023–25 period was a margin-expansion period for software, not a competitive squeeze. That contradicts the "AI increases vendor costs" half of the counter-story, at least so far and at least for incumbents. Costs fell relative to revenue.
- Combined with the PPI series in section 1, the picture is: software vendors raised prices and cut costs as a share of revenue. That is the opposite of deflationary for buyers, and it is not "competition driving costs up" either. It is pricing power.
- Caveats: this is an incumbent panel (survivors with 7+ years of filings). Private AI-native firms, whose costs are the ones widely reported to be rising, are not in it. Stock-based compensation is included in the expense lines. Oracle is excluded from gross margin because it does not tag a total cost of revenue.

List prices (hand-compiled, `data/manual/saas_list_prices.csv`): every tracked incumbent SaaS plan except GitHub Team is more expensive today than when first observed. Microsoft 365 E3 went from $20 to $36 (2022) and $66 with Copilot. Salesforce Enterprise $125 → $175. Adobe CC $49.99 → $69.99. Slack Pro $6.67 → $8.75. The AI features have mostly shipped as paid add-ons or bundled into higher tiers, not as price cuts.

The one clearly deflationary series is LLM API token prices (`charts/4_llm_prices.png`): frontier-class output tokens went from $60/M (GPT-4, 2023) to $8–10/M (GPT-4.1/GPT-5 class, 2025), roughly an 80–90% drop in two years, with cheap tiers below $1/M. This is an input price for software vendors, not a price consumers pay.

## 3. Reliability of major services (chart: `charts/1_incidents.png`)

Status-page incidents per year (excluding scheduled maintenance), before vs after:

| provider | 2019–2022 avg | 2023–2025 avg | change | comparable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cloudflare | 379 | 699 | +84% | yes, same API whole period |
| GitHub | 84 | 162 | +94% | yes, same statuspage whole period |
| Datadog | 58 | 32 | -44% | yes |
| Google Cloud | 120 | 258 (2023–24) | +114% | partial: feed format changed 2021 and 2025 |
| AWS | 107 | n/a | n/a | old dashboard feed ends March 2023 |

Major/critical-severity incidents per year: GitHub 1 → 36 (2019 → 2025), Cloudflare flat around 40, Datadog 26 → 15.

What this does and does not say:

- Cloudflare and GitHub, the two providers with clean, unchanged data feeds across the whole period, both show roughly a doubling of reported incidents after 2022, and GitHub's major-incident count rose every year. Datadog moved the other way.
- Incident counts are a measure of what a company chooses to post. A provider that adds more products, lowers its posting threshold, or automates status posts will show more incidents without being less reliable. Cloudflare in particular posts per-datacentre notices. Total incident-hours (sum of durations) rose in the same direction for Cloudflare, GCP and GitHub, so it is not only more, shorter posts.
- Nothing in this data shows reliability improving at any of the five. The "AI makes software better" claim, measured this way, has no support; the weaker claim "reliability did not get worse" is contradicted for 3 of 4 measurable providers.

## 4. How big a price drop would the AI spend need to buy? (chart: `charts/5_ai_spend.png`)

The other side of the ledger. "Deflationary" has to be net: AI is itself a large new stream of spending, on chips, buildings and power. For it to be deflationary on net, the price cuts it produces elsewhere have to be at least as large as the money it adds.

Spend, from 10-K cash-flow statements (capital expenditure, $bn, fiscal years). Each number is the `PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment` fact (Amazon: `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets`) in the company's SEC XBRL feed, linked in the header; NVIDIA is total revenue:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 16.9 | 23.5 | 13.9 | 15.1 | 1.7 | 71 | — | 11.7 |

| 2023 | 52.7 | 32.3 | 28.1 | 27.0 | 8.7 | 149 | -6 | 27.0 |

Microsoft (FY ends June), Oracle (May) and NVIDIA (January) have already reported FY2026: $116bn, $56bn and $216bn respectively, so the FY2026 total will be well above FY2025's.

Break-even. Take the capex above the FY2022 level as the AI buildout (this understates it: it ignores CoreWeave-type neoclouds, xAI, sovereign builds, and the power and construction spend outside these five). Set it against what Americans spend in a year (BEA national accounts, nominal, 2025 average of seasonally adjusted annual rates, via FRED):

| FY2025 | $bn | incremental AI capex as % |
|---|---|---|
| Incremental capex, five companies | 224 | |
| US consumer spending on goods ([PCE goods, DGDSRC1](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGDSRC1)) | 6,508 | **3.4%** |
| US consumer spending, all ([PCEC](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCEC)) | 20,955 | 1.1% |
| US GDP ([GDP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/GDP)) | 30,762 | 0.7% |
| US business investment in information-processing equipment ([Y033RC1Q027SBEA](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/Y033RC1Q027SBEA)) | 1,644 | 13.6% |

What "0.7% of GDP" is and is not. It is capex above each company's own FY2022 level, summed over five companies. It is not a measurement of AI spending. It misses every buyer of NVIDIA hardware other than these five (CoreWeave and other neoclouds, xAI, sovereign and enterprise buyers): NVIDIA's FY2025 revenue of $130bn, with chips typically around half of a data centre's cost, points to a total buildout in the $300–400bn range, i.e. 1.0–1.3% of GDP. In the other direction, some of the growth over FY2022 is ordinary cloud and logistics expansion, not AI. Total capex of the five, without netting out 2022, is $379bn, 1.2% of GDP. Reasonable range: 0.7–1.3% of GDP for 2025, rising sharply in 2026.

Is that big? Compared with earlier US buildouts, yes; it is in the top tier but not unprecedented. Estimates of the historical episodes differ a lot depending on what is counted, so two sources are shown:

| buildout (peak years) | % of US GDP per year | source |
|---|---|---|
| Railroads, 1865–1890 | 2.4 (avg); ~6 at the 1880s peak by another count | [NY Fed AMEC symposium deck, Apr 2026](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/conference/2026/amec-symposium-on-the-k-shaped-economy/presentations/5_20260403-k-shaped-markus.pdf); [Investment Research Partners](https://www.investmentresearchpartners.com/post/chart-of-the-week-8-3-2025) |
| Electrification, 1905–1925 | 1.1 | NY Fed deck |
| Interstate highways, 1956–1973 | 1.6 | NY Fed deck |
| Telecom / fibre, 1996–2003 | 0.8 (avg); ~1.0 at the 2000 peak | NY Fed deck; Investment Research Partners |
| AI data centres, 2025 | 1.2 (IRP estimate); 0.7–1.3 (this repo, five-company incremental to total) | see above |
| AI, 2025–2032 projection | 2.8 | NY Fed deck |

For scale on the other axis: all US public infrastructure spending (federal, state, local; highways, transit, water) ran at 2.3% of GDP in 2023 ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/four-recent-trends-in-us-public-infrastructure-spending/)). China's economic-infrastructure investment averaged 8.6% of GDP over 1992–2013 by [McKinsey Global Institute's count](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/bridging-global-infrastructure-gaps) (2016, exhibit 2), versus 2.5% for the US over the same period; that is a whole-economy, state-directed investment model rather than one buildout, and is not comparable with the private episodes above.

Two things the analogues say. The 1990s IT wave did show up in measured US productivity, about a percentage point a year over 1995–2004, but a decade after the spending started. And the two closest private analogues, railroads and telecom, both ended with output prices (freight rates, bandwidth) collapsing because the capital was overbuilt, not because the technology paid off as planned. That is a deflationary outcome, but not the one the "AI is deflationary" argument has in mind.

Reading: for the AI buildout to be net-neutral in dollar terms in 2025, the price of every physical good Americans buy — food, cars, clothes, fuel, furniture, phones — would have had to fall by about 3.4% relative to where it otherwise would have been, in that one year, with volumes unchanged. Against total consumption it is about 1.1%. If FY2026 capex lands where the three early reporters suggest, the required drop roughly doubles.

The direct price series in section 1 show none of that. Goods-linked series that fell (world food and energy commodities) fell for reasons that predate the buildout, and the ones most tied to the buildout (electricity, metals) rose. Note also that this is a flow comparison — dollars spent this year against dollars that would need to be saved this year — not a claim about what the capital stock eventually yields. The deflationary case has to be that the savings arrive later. That may be true. It is not "obvious", and it is not yet in the data.

Caveats: capex figures are total capex, not AI-only (Amazon's includes warehouses and logistics; the FY2022 baseline removes most of that). Fiscal years are not aligned to calendar years. BEA denominators are nominal annual averages. The counterfactual "what prices otherwise would have been" is not observable; the percentages are the size of the effect that would have to exist, not a measurement of one.

## 5. Is the effect visible where it should be? Difference tests (chart: `charts/6_productivity.png`)

Aggregate productivity cannot answer the question: even a 10–15% return on 0.7% of GDP is about 0.1% of GDP a year, inside the noise of the national series. What can be tested is a *difference*: if AI is raising output or cutting prices, that should show up first in the industries that use it most, relative to industries that barely can — same country, same rates, same tariffs, same commodity shocks, so the common macro noise cancels. Three such comparisons, all from output-per-hour or price series rather than CPI (`scripts/fetch_productivity.py`, `data/processed/productivity.csv`). The BLS and OECD series are government-produced; they are used here for output-per-hour and for cross-country comparison, not as the primary evidence on prices.

**Output per hour, AI-exposed vs unexposed industries** ([BLS detailed industry labor productivity](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/tables/), annual, index 2019 = 100, median of the group, balanced to the series that run to 2025). Exposed: software publishers, commercial banking, engineering services, publishing, travel agencies (five more — CPA offices, tax preparation, architecture, advertising, employment placement — stop at 2023 and are excluded from the chart). Unexposed: trucking, restaurants, hotels, auto repair, grocery, electric power, warehousing, couriers, gas stations, car dealers.

| group | 2019 | 2022 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| exposed (n=5) | 100 | 103 | 129 |
| unexposed (n=10) | 100 | 99 | 99 |

The gap opens after 2022 and is large. Read it with care: n=5, and the 2019 base flatters anything that collapsed in 2020 (travel agencies +62% 2019→2025 is a COVID rebound, not AI). Software publishers alone: +29% 2019→2025, +26% 2022→2025. Banking and engineering: about +10% each. This is consistent with AI raising output per hour in software-heavy industries; it is equally consistent with the 2023 tech layoffs (fewer hours, same revenue). The series cannot tell those apart.

**Producer prices, exposed ÷ unexposed** (BLS PPI, monthly; exposed: software publishers, hosting, CPA, law, consulting, engineering, advertising, employment services; unexposed: trucking, hotels, hospitals, electric power, insurance). The ratio was 100 in 2019, 97.6 in 2022, 96.4 in Aug 2026. Exposed services' prices are rising about 1pp/yr slower than unexposed ones, but the drift started in 2020, before ChatGPT, and the only exposed series whose price has actually fallen since 2022 is management consulting (−7%). Law-office prices are up 33% since 2022. Relative disinflation, yes; absolute price cuts in AI-exposed services, no.

**US vs peers, real GDP per hour** ([OECD](https://data-explorer.oecd.org/), 2019 = 100). US 112 in 2025; euro area 102, UK 102, Japan 104, Germany 102, Canada 103. The US gap is real and it widened after 2022 (US +5.9 points 2019→2022, +5.9 more 2022→2025; peers roughly flat after 2022). But the US was already pulling away in 2020–21, and the usual explanations — fiscal stimulus, energy prices, labour-market churn, the mix of who left the workforce — predate the buildout. Timing is consistent with an AI contribution; it does not identify one.

Net: the difference tests point the same direction as the hypothesis (the exposed side is more productive and its relative prices are softer), but nothing in them separates AI from the 2023 cost-cutting cycle, and none of it has reached the prices buyers pay in section 1.

## Where this leaves the hypothesis

"AI is obviously deflationary" is not supported by any public series we could find that consumers or business buyers actually pay:

- Consumer-facing prices we tracked without CPI (housing, rent, streaming, electricity) are all still rising after 2022. Commodity food and energy fell, for reasons that predate AI.
- The price software sellers receive rose after 25 years of gentle decline. Incumbent SaaS list prices went up, with AI sold as an add-on.
- Software vendors' margins widened; costs as a share of revenue fell. So the specific mechanism proposed here — competition raising vendor costs — is not what the incumbents' filings show. Pricing power is.
- Reliability, where measurable, got worse or stayed flat.
- The buildout itself added about $224bn of new spending in 2025 from five companies alone; offsetting that would have taken a 3.4% drop in the price of all US goods. Nothing close to that is visible.

The only strongly deflationary series is the price of LLM tokens, an intermediate input. So far that deflation has been captured as vendor margin and spent on hardware and energy, not passed through to buyers.

What would change this conclusion: gross margins of the panel turning down as inference cost grows; software PPI rolling over; incident rates at Cloudflare/GitHub falling back; or evidence that non-software output prices (goods, services) decelerated in a way that can be tied to AI adoption rather than to rate cycles and commodity reversals. The scripts here will pick that up as it happens — re-run `make all`.

## Known weaknesses

- Correlation with a date, not causation. Nov 2022 also coincides with rate rises, the end of ZIRP, and the tech layoffs of 2023; margin expansion is plausibly mostly that.
- Incident data reflects disclosure policy as much as reliability. AWS and GCP history is incomplete.
- Streaming, SaaS and LLM prices are hand-compiled from announcements; each row carries a source URL but they have not been independently re-verified.
- The SEC panel is survivor-biased toward incumbents. AI-native private companies are the ones whose costs are reported as rising, and they are absent.
- Three years of "after" data.
- Section 5's exposed productivity group is five series. The BLS series for the most AI-exposed white-collar services stop at 2023.
